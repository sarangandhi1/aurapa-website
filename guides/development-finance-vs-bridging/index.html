Development finance or bridging: which fits which project?

The dividing line is the scale of the works. Cosmetic and light refurbishment sits with bridging, heavy refurbishment sits with either depending on cost, and structural work, conversions and ground-up builds sit with development finance. The products differ in how the money arrives: a bridge pays out in one advance, while development finance is drawn in stages against build progress, which changes both the monitoring and the interest bill.

Where lenders draw the line

Lenders sort projects into three rough bands. Light refurbishment means no structural work and no change of use: new kitchen and bathroom, rewiring, redecoration. This is standard bridging territory. Heavy refurbishment involves structural elements or a change of use, for example a loft dormer, an extension or converting a house to flats, and sits with refurbishment bridges or small development facilities depending on cost. Ground-up development, building from a cleared or bare site, is development finance. A common rule of thumb is that works costing more than about 15% of the property value start to push a project out of light refurbishment, though each lender defines its own bands.

How the two products pay out

A bridging loan completes in a single advance. The lender takes a first charge, the borrower receives the net advance on day one and manages the works alone. Interest, typically 0.75% to 1.1% a month, is charged on the full gross loan from day one, usually retained or rolled rather than serviced.

Development finance splits the facility in two. The land or site element is advanced on day one, commonly up to 60% to 70% of the site value. The build element is drawn in stages, in arrears, against inspections by the lender's monitoring surveyor: foundations, wall plate, wind and watertight, and so on. Interest is only charged on the balance actually drawn, which is the key economic difference, and facilities are sized against two caps, typically 60% to 70% of gross development value and 80% to 90% of total cost.

A worked example: £355,000 for a ground-up build

Take a single house plot bought for £150,000 with build costs of £250,000 and a gross development value of £550,000.

ItemFigure
Site purchase£150,000
Day one advance at 70% of site value£105,000
Build facility drawn in stages£250,000
Total facility£355,000
Gross development value£550,000
Loan to GDVroughly 65%

£355,000 against £550,000 is 64.5% of GDV, inside a typical cap. Now the interest difference. If the whole £355,000 sat drawn for 12 months at 0.9% a month, interest would be £355,000 x 0.009 x 12 = £38,340. Drawn in stages, the average balance might be around £230,000, giving interest of about £230,000 x 0.009 x 12 = £24,840, roughly £13,500 less on the same project. Staged drawdown is not free, since monitoring surveyor fees of a few thousand pounds over the build offset part of the saving, but on larger builds the interest saved dominates. Why using the wrong product costs money

Funding a genuine build with a big bridge means paying interest on money that sits idle for months, as the example shows. The opposite mistake is slower but also real: putting a simple refurbishment through development finance adds monitoring surveyor fees, staged paperwork and drawdown delays that a £40,000 kitchen and bathroom project does not need. Bridging also completes faster, in one to three weeks against typically four to eight for development finance, which matters when a site is bought under auction conditions with a 28 day completion deadline. Some projects use both in sequence: a bridge to secure the site quickly, refinanced onto a development facility once planning improves.

What lenders look at beyond the numbers

Development lenders underwrite the borrower's track record as heavily as the site. A first project with no build experience usually needs a fixed price contract with an established contractor, a lower loan to cost, or both. Planning status matters: full consent is bankable, outline consent restricts the day one advance, and no consent means the site is effectively a land bridge. Contingency is expected, typically 10% of build costs, and its absence in an appraisal is a common reason for a reduced facility.

Both products stand or fall on the exit

Whichever product fits, the loan ends the same way: the completed project is sold or refinanced. Development lenders test the GDV against local comparables and the sales period against the local market, exactly as bridging lenders test the exit on a bridge. On the worked example, the £355,000 facility is repaid from a £550,000 sale, or from a retained scheme, by a term mortgage against the completed value. Lenders typically decide marginal cases on the strength of that exit evidence rather than on the rate the borrower is willing to pay.

Related questions

At what point does a refurbishment become a development?

Broadly when the works are structural or involve a change of use: extensions, loft conversions with dormers, converting a house into flats, or anything needing full planning consent. Many lenders also use cost as a marker, with works above roughly 15% to 25% of the property value pushing a case out of the light refurbishment bracket.

Why is interest lower on development finance if the rates look similar?

Because interest is only charged on the balance actually drawn. The build element is released in stages against surveyor inspections, so the average drawn balance over the project is well below the facility total. On the £355,000 example, staged drawdown saves roughly £13,500 of interest over 12 months against a fully drawn loan at the same rate.

What does a monitoring surveyor actually do?

The lender appoints a surveyor to inspect the site before each drawdown, confirm the works claimed are complete and that the remaining facility can finish the build, then certify the release. Borrowers pay for each inspection, typically a few hundred pounds per visit, and builds usually involve four to eight visits.

Can I get development finance for my first project?

A workable minority of lenders take first time developers, usually at a lower loan to cost and with conditions, most commonly a fixed price build contract with an experienced contractor. A track record of completed projects widens the lender panel and improves pricing more than almost any other factor.

How long does development finance take to arrange?

Typically four to eight weeks from application to first drawdown, against one to three weeks for a bridge. The extra time goes on the appraisal, the monitoring surveyor's initial report and legal work on the site. Where a site has to be secured quickly, some borrowers bridge the purchase first and refinance onto the development facility.
